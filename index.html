my brother says love is a violet Ophelia !Rosemary Act I, Sc. iii No.1601

>be me
>daughter of Polonius
>Lord Hamlet has been sending me letters and gifts
>brother leaving for France, stops to lecture me first

So before Laertes leaves, he sits me down for a talk about Hamlet. According to him, Hamlet's love is just "a violet in the youth of primy nature, forward, not permanent, sweet, not lasting." Basically: nice while it lasts, but it won't. He also reminded me that a prince doesn't get to pick his own wife, so I should keep my guard up.

Then my dad walked in and it got worse. I told him Hamlet has always courted me "in honourable fashion," and he actually laughed. He called Hamlet's promises "springes to catch woodcocks," which is a fancy way of saying traps for dumb birds. When he asked what I think, all I could come up with was "I do not know, my lord, what I should think." So that's that. I'm not allowed to talk to Hamlet anymore.

Margin Note ## Analysis No.1605

In Shakespeare's time, the violet stood for faithfulness, and this scene shows what kind of faithfulness Ophelia is expected to have. She defends Hamlet once, then backs down. Her lines get smaller as the scene goes on: first a defense, then "I do not know, my lord, what I should think," and finally just "I shall obey, my lord" (1.3). She wants to stay loyal to both Hamlet and her father, but the play makes her choose, and she chooses obedience. Even the violet that describes her love comes from her brother, not from her. From the very first scene, other people speak for her.

>dad and the King want to know if love is what's wrong with Hamlet
>they set it up so Hamlet "accidentally" runs into me
>they hide and listen

I gave his gifts back and told him, "Rich gifts wax poor when givers prove unkind." First he said he loved me once. Then he said he never did. Then he told me to go to a nunnery. I honestly don't know which version of him I was talking to.

After he left, I just stood there thinking about who he used to be. He was "the expectancy and rose of the fair state," the one everyone looked up to. "O, what a noble mind is here o'erthrown!" And then there's me, "of ladies most deject and wretched, that sucked the honey of his music vows." I believed every one of those vows.

Margin Note ## Analysis No.1611

The rose stood for love and perfection, and here it stands for Ophelia's devotion to Hamlet. He has just insulted her and said he never loved her, but once he leaves, she grieves for him before herself. She calls him "the expectancy and rose of the fair state" (3.1) and only mentions her own pain after that. She stays loyal to him even after he turns on her. Meanwhile, her father treats her heartbreak as useful information: "we heard it all" (3.1). Nobody asks how she feels. She was used as bait, and then her pain was ignored.

Margin Note ## Analysis No.1616

After her father is killed and buried in secret, Ophelia's mind breaks. For the first time, she is the one handing out flowers instead of being described by them. Rosemary meant remembrance, which is exactly what her father was denied. Rue meant sorrow and regret, and it is the only plant she keeps for herself: "here's some for me" (4.5). The violets from her first thread, the flower of faithfulness, have "withered all." The two things she was faithful to, her father and Hamlet, are both gone. When she was sane, people told her what to think. Now that she is mad, she finally says what she feels, in the language of flowers.

Flower of the day Willow: fragility and forsaken love

Laertes Act IV, Sc. vii No.1618

Too much of water hast thou, poor Ophelia, and therefore I forbid my tears. (4.7)

Margin Note ## Analysis No.1619

The willow was the tree of forsaken love, and it fits Ophelia's fragility. Like a willow branch, she bends instead of fighting back, and in the end she bends right into the water. She doesn't even get to tell the story of her own death. It happens offstage and Gertrude describes it, which is why someone else posts this thread. The play also never says for sure whether she meant to die. Gertrude makes it sound like an accident, but later the Priest calls her death "doubtful" (5.1). She floats there "incapable of her own distress" (4.7), just as powerless at the end as she was the whole play.

Margin Note ## Analysis No.1627

The violet comes back at the end. In her first thread, Laertes used a violet to warn her about Hamlet. Now he hopes violets will grow from her "fair and unpolluted flesh" (5.1). From his first speech to his last, what he cares about most is her purity. At the funeral, Laertes and Hamlet fight in her grave over who loved her more, and Hamlet claims "forty thousand brothers" couldn't match him. Once she can't answer, everyone finally talks about Ophelia, and loudly. Her board ends the same way her life did: taken over and shut down by other people.
