Margin Note ## Analysis No.1605

In Shakespeare's time the violet stood for faithfulness, and this scene shows which kind of faithfulness wins. Ophelia defends Hamlet once, then gives up her own judgment completely. Her words shrink from a defense, to "I do not know, my lord, what I should think," to "I shall obey, my lord" (1.3). Her agenda is to keep both her love for Hamlet and her duty to her father, but the play won't let her have both. Even the flower describing her love comes from her brother's mouth, not hers. From the start, other people speak for her.

How now, Ophelia? You need not tell us what Lord Hamlet said; we heard it all. (3.1)

Margin Note ## Analysis No.1611

The rose meant love and perfection, and it stands for Ophelia's devotion. Hamlet has just insulted her and denied ever loving her, yet her speech mourns him before herself. She calls him "the expectancy and rose of the fair state" (3.1) and only then mentions her own pain. Her love stays loyal even after he abandons it. Meanwhile her father treats her heartbreak as information: "we heard it all." No one asks how she feels. She was used as bait, and her grief is ignored.

Margin Note ## Analysis No.1616

Ophelia's father is dead and was buried in secret, and her mind breaks. For the first time she is the one handing out flowers. Rosemary meant remembrance, which her father was denied. Rue meant sorrow and regret, and it is the only plant she keeps for herself: "here's some for me" (4.5). The violets from her first thread, the flower of faithfulness, have "withered all." Both things she was faithful to, her father and her love, are gone. Sane, she was told what to think. Mad, she finally says what she feels, in the language of flowers.

Flower of the day: Willow, fragility and forsaken love

Laertes Act IV, Sc. vii No.1618

Too much of water hast thou, poor Ophelia, and therefore I forbid my tears. (4.7)

Margin Note ## Analysis No.1619

The willow was the tree of forsaken love, and it stands for Ophelia's fragility: like its branches, she bends instead of resisting, and in the end she bends into the water. Even her death is not hers to tell. It happens offstage and Gertrude narrates it, which is why this thread is posted by someone else. The play never settles whether she meant to die. Gertrude's account sounds like an accident, but the Priest later calls her death "doubtful" (5.1). She floats "incapable of her own distress" (4.7), as powerless at the end as she was all along.

Margin Note ## Analysis No.1627

The violet returns and closes the circle. In her first thread Laertes used a violet to warn her about Hamlet. Now he wishes violets out of her "unpolluted flesh" (5.1). From his first speech to his last, he defines her by her purity. At her funeral, Laertes and Hamlet fight in her grave over who loved her more, and Hamlet claims "forty thousand brothers" could not match him. Everyone finally talks about Ophelia, loudly, once she can no longer answer. Her board ends the way her life did: taken over and closed by other people.
